B · How the service works

Zhexuan & Anmol · 18 min

The service sits between your system and the cloud assistant. It never calls the cloud itself and needs no outbound network access — what happens to the redacted text afterwards is entirely your system's decision. Collapsed sections are for reference after the meeting; we will walk the main line only.

1 · The shape of it

Two calls, not one
Your system posts the user's task plus the email — subject, sender, recipients, body, quoted thread. We return the same content with private spans replaced by placeholders, plus one decision for the request as a whole. Later, separately, you post the cloud's reply back and we restore the real values. The mapping from placeholder to original never leaves our machine.
Restore is deliberate, never automatic
Nothing is restored unless you ask for it, by reference id, as its own call. That call bypasses detection entirely — it only scans the reply and substitutes back.
You keep control of the cloud
We do not hold your API keys, we are not tied to your cloud provider, and we never make an outbound call. Whether the redacted text goes to the cloud at all is your system's decision, made after we hand it back.

2 · Five design principles

PrincipleWhat it means in practice
Fail closedAny error means the content was not processed. A successful response whose decision is not "ready" carries the same consequence: do not forward it. If any component fails, nothing is released.
Regulated categories are gated by policy, not by scoreFor medical, financial, credential and business-confidential data, a confidence score never authorises release, however high. Only the approved policy does.
No request waits on a personWhen the model is unsure, the span is masked anyway and recorded as uncertain. Nothing blocks on human review.
No sensitive loggingInput text, detected values and restored text are never written to logs — only ids, counts, timings, decisions and error types.
Local onlyNo outbound network calls. Models and lists are installed at deployment; the running service does not reach the internet.

Bearer auth on everything except the health checks. All errors are RFC 9457 problem details, carrying a type, title, status and request id.

Detail: what comes back for each span

Every span we act on is reported with:

  • Entity type — phone, person, organisation…
  • Category — which sensitivity category it fell into
  • Confidence — how sure the model was
  • Which detector found it — a rule, entity recognition, or the model
  • A short reason

Never the original value. The response tells you what was done and why, but reading it does not disclose anything.

The request-level decision is separate from the spans: it covers the whole task plus email plus thread, and it is not simply an aggregate of the individual scores.

C · Deciding what to redact

Yudi · 20 min

Two designs presented together, because they are not alternatives — one is the baseline the other has to beat. Seventeen papers behind this; sources on expand.

1 · The baseline: mask everything we find

Start by not deciding at all. If any detector flags a span, it is replaced with a reversible placeholder. The public support line gets masked too. Nothing is judged and nothing is argued about.

Why this is a serious starting point, not a straw man
Masking a public phone number used to be a mistake because the information was lost. Here it is only hidden from the cloud and comes back in the final answer. So over-redaction stops being a visible error and becomes a small, measurable drop in the cloud's reasoning quality. The hardest judgement call in the project turns into a tuning knob.
The evidence it rests on
A September 2026 study tested five anonymisation styles on a reasoning benchmark: consistent placeholders scored the same as the original text, while blanking out lost about a quarter of the score. That is the whole argument for placeholders over redaction marks. The same study warns that lookup-heavy tasks degrade badly even with placeholders, which is why section E asks how often the executive asks about outside companies.
And it is the control condition
Masking everything sets the ceiling on safety and the floor on usefulness. Every refinement after this has to earn its place by measurement — how much answer quality it buys back, and how much leakage it costs. Starting strict and loosening with evidence is easier to stand behind than starting loose.

2 · Why one judgement is not enough

What the research foundSo we
Sensitive is not the same as personal. "Exit the regional-bank book before the Fed meeting" names no person and discloses the whole strategyProtect what the executive is doing, not only who is named
One on/off gate breaks the assistant. Sensitivity alone kept 4% of the content a task needs; adding a second judgement kept 95%Two judgements, not one threshold
Detection scores are not privacy scores. 9% of records were re-identified after every detected identifier had been maskedReport leak rate, never plain detector accuracy
Context cannot be scored reliably. Human reviewers agree only 48% of the time on context-dependent sensitivityFix as much as possible by written policy, and keep the judging surface small
No single detector is good enough. The best open-source detector averaged 0.54 across four datasets, and each failed on a different kind of textRun several and take the union

That 48% figure is why the design pushes as much as possible onto things that cannot be wrong — where the text came from, whether it matches a checksum, and what a written policy says — and leaves the model only what genuinely needs reading.

3 · The refinement: two judgements, three actions

JudgementWhat it asksWhere the answer comes from
CategoryWhat kind of thing is this?Rules, checksums, entity recognition, then the model
May a score release it?Can confidence alone authorise sending this?The approved policy. For five categories the answer is never

The five that a score may never release: medical, financial accounts, government IDs, credentials, business-confidential. Everything else is score-driven, with a strict bar for personal details and a looser one for public ones.

Basis. Impact levels follow NIST SP 800-122, which classifies personal data by the harm disclosure would cause and notes that combinations of fields can be more sensitive than any single field. The rule that loose-bar items tighten when several appear together follows Sweeney's finding that ZIP code, gender and date of birth uniquely identify an estimated 87% of the US population.

Known limitation: NIST SP 800-122 covers personal data only, so the tiering for business information will follow your own internal data classification rather than an external standard.

Detail: business-confidential, the one category we cannot detect

The other four regulated categories have a format or a clear meaning. This one has neither — a project code name looks like an ordinary word. Coverage rests on three layers:

LayerHow it worksCatches
1 · Term listYou supply project code names, deal names and key counterparties; any match is redactedWhat you already know is confidential
2 · Explicit markersPhrases such as "confidential", "board only", "do not forward" or "privileged" raise the surrounding passage to the highest tierWhat the sender marked
3 · Default placeholderProper nouns and amounts the model cannot classify are replaced with a reversible placeholder rather than sentWhat the first two layers missed

Layer 1 needs the term list from you. This category depends most on the model's judgement and is therefore the main recall risk; its leak rate will be reported separately rather than averaged into the others.

4 · How the bar gets set

The thresholds are not ours to pick. You set an acceptable leak rate per category — that is a business decision. We then measure leak rate and over-redaction across a range of thresholds on the labelled emails, and take the highest threshold that still meets your target, so nothing is redacted unnecessarily.

Illustrative shape only, not measured results. With a 2% target the selected threshold would be 0.3. This table is what we will hand you once the dataset exists, with real numbers, so the trade-off is yours to make rather than ours.

Detail: the confidence score, and why it has to be calibrated

Language models are consistently overconfident when asked how sure they are, so the raw score is not used directly.

Method 1, calibration. Run the system on the labelled emails, group spans by raw score, and measure what proportion in each group was actually private. At run time the raw score is converted to that observed proportion. A raw 0.95 might be reported as 0.70. This makes a threshold of 0.5 mean an observed 50% likelihood, rather than whatever the model felt.

Method 2, agreement — the fallback. If calibration shows the scores barely discriminate, for instance if nearly everything comes back near 0.9, the score is derived instead from agreement: what share of detectors flagged the span, and what share of repeated model runs called it private. Agreement across repeated answers is a more reliable uncertainty signal than a stated confidence. Both will be compared on the same data.

Detail: when the model is unsure

It masks anyway, and records that it was unsure. No request waits on a person.

The reasoning: human review exists because masking the wrong thing is expensive. With reversible placeholders it is cheap — the value comes back in the final answer. Since it is cheap, it does not justify a person's time, and an assistant used dozens of times a day cannot pause for one.

Those uncertainty records are also how we measure over-redaction, so the cost of caution is a number rather than a guess.

Detail: how we will prove it works
What we measureHow
Leak rate — the headlineShare of private spans that reached the cloud, reported per category rather than averaged
Over-redactionShare of public spans masked unnecessarily
Usability costAnswer quality with redaction on versus off — this is what privacy costs, as a number
CalibrationWhether a reported confidence matches the observed proportion
LatencyProcessing time per email on the confirmed hardware

Ablation. Starting from the baseline, components are added one at a time — detector union, then entity propagation, then repeated runs, then the category bars — so each one's contribution is visible separately. Thresholds are tuned on one subset and reported on a held-out subset, so the numbers are not flattered.

Evasion tests. Look-alike characters and zero-width characters inside identifiers, encoded account numbers, values split across lines, values inside tables where recognition degrades. Plus one test specific to this design: a message carrying hidden text that instructs the assistant to include the contact list.

Detail: what is ours and what is borrowed

No individual component here is novel; each has a published source or an industry precedent. What is ours is the integration for executive email without per-item human review — the closest prior system requires the user to confirm every item, which an assistant used daily cannot do — plus a labelled email benchmark and the ablation that quantifies each component.

The honest risk: that 0.63 recall figure means an 8B model missed roughly a third of personal data. Rescriber's users reviewed every message; here a miss reaches the cloud directly. Raising recall is therefore the main engineering effort, measured from a baseline rather than assumed.
